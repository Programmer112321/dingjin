Process-Based Models

1 · Introduction

Process-based models (PBMs) represent ecosystem and environmental behavior through explicit mathematical formulations of physical, chemical, and biological processes. Rather than fitting empirical relationships to observed data, PBMs encode mechanistic understanding of how and why systems function — using differential equations and energy/mass balance constraints derived from first principles. This makes them uniquely suited to extrapolation beyond observed conditions, scenario analysis, and the study of cross-scale feedbacks that purely statistical methods cannot recover.

Advantages

  • Mechanistic interpretability — parameters correspond to measurable physical and biological quantities (Vcmax, aerodynamic roughness length, decomposition rates), making outputs traceable and falsifiable
  • Extrapolation to novel conditions — mechanistic constraints prevent unrealistic predictions outside the observational range; critical for climate-change projections
  • Captures feedbacks — vegetation–climate–soil and urban canopy–atmosphere feedbacks are embedded in the model structure, not inferred post hoc
  • Enables scenario analysis — a consistent framework for comparing SSP126 vs. SSP585, management interventions, green infrastructure designs, or disturbance regimes
  • Process attribution — individual process contributions (e.g., transpiration vs. albedo cooling; GPP vs. respiration) can be isolated by turning model components on or off

Disadvantages

  • Parameter uncertainty — many parameters (Vcmax, turnover times, aerodynamic roughness, allocation fractions) are poorly constrained globally and require calibration
  • Structural uncertainty — different formulations of the same process (e.g., stomatal conductance schemes) produce divergent predictions even with identical inputs
  • Computational cost — global DGVMs at fine spatial resolution and 3D urban CFD models both require substantial computing resources
  • Missing processes — nutrient cycling, mycorrhizal networks, hydraulic failure under drought, and fine-scale urban morphology are still poorly represented in many models
  • Rigid assumptions — fixed plant functional types and parameterizations may not capture local adaptation or rapid vegetation change

2 · Vegetation Process-Based Models

Vegetation PBMs simulate the coupled cycles of carbon, water, energy, and nutrients across scales ranging from individual tree plots to the global land surface. At their core, photosynthesis (GPP) follows the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry (FvCB) biochemical scheme; GPP minus autotrophic respiration yields NPP; NPP minus heterotrophic decomposition yields NEE. Water balance links Penman–Monteith evapotranspiration to soil moisture and runoff. At larger scales, vegetation composition and structure are themselves dynamic state variables that respond to and feed back on climate.

Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs)

DGVMs are the flagship tool for projecting terrestrial ecosystem responses to climate change at regional to global scales. They represent vegetation as a mixture of Plant Functional Types (PFTs) — broadleaf deciduous trees, C4 grasses, boreal conifers, etc. — each characterized by distinct photosynthetic, phenological, and hydraulic parameters. Within each grid cell, PFTs compete for light, water, and nutrients, and their fractional cover evolves through growth, mortality, and disturbance.

Core processes simulated:

Process Key formulation
Leaf-level photosynthesis FvCB scheme (Vcmax, Jmax, mesophyll conductance)
Stomatal conductance Ball–Berry or Medlyn optimization framework
Carbon allocation Fixed or dynamic partitioning to leaf, wood, root, and seed pools
Phenology Temperature / day-length / chilling-day thresholds
Mortality Carbon starvation, hydraulic failure, background turnover
Disturbance Fire (SPITFIRE), gap dynamics, land-use change

Major models in this family include LPJ-GUESS, CLM-FATES, ED2, ORCHIDEE, and JULES. They form the core of the TRENDY multi-model ensemble (used in the Global Carbon Budget) and are the land surface components of CMIP6 Earth System Models (ESMs) driving SSP126–SSP585 projections. Best suited for long-term climate impact assessment, regional carbon budget attribution, and land–atmosphere feedback studies.

Forest Gap Models

Gap models — including SORTIE and FORMIND — simulate individual tree birth, growth, competition for light, and mortality within small plots (~0.01–1 ha). They resolve size-structured competition and successional dynamics over decades to centuries following disturbance (fire, logging, insect outbreak). Best suited for stand-scale biodiversity–productivity analysis and forest succession studies.

3-PG — Physiological Principles Predicting Growth

3-PG is a parsimonious process-based model driven by monthly climate inputs (temperature, precipitation, radiation, VPD) and soil water-holding capacity. It simulates forest stand growth and biomass production using a light-use efficiency (fAPAR × ε) framework modified by temperature, VPD, and water-stress modifiers. Widely used in plantation forestry and timber yield estimation due to its low data requirements and strong empirical validation base.

3 · Cooling Effect of Urban Green Space — Process-Based Models

Urban green spaces cool cities through three coupled physical pathways: (1) evapotranspiration — latent heat flux removes energy as water vapor; (2) shading — tree canopies intercept solar and longwave radiation, reducing surface and air temperatures; and (3) airflow and convective heat exchange — vegetation modifies wind speed, turbulence, and the aerodynamic coupling between surfaces and the overlying atmosphere, governing how efficiently sensible heat is transported away. These three pathways interact: dense canopies that maximize shading can simultaneously reduce wind speed and suppress convective cooling, while open park configurations may enhance advective cooling from cooler air into surrounding streets.

Process-based models vary in how explicitly they resolve each pathway. CFD-based models (ENVI-met) fully couple all three; energy-balance models (SUEWS, TRM) parameterize convection through aerodynamic resistance; mesoscale models (WRF-Urban, CLMU) resolve city-to-atmosphere feedbacks. Inventory-based tools (i-Tree Eco) estimate cooling from leaf area and microclimate records.

ENVI-met

A 3D microscale model (0.5–5 m resolution) that solves fully coupled atmospheric dynamics, radiation transfer, and soil–vegetation equations. It explicitly resolves turbulent airflow around building facades and within tree canopies using a k-ε turbulence closure, computes convective heat exchange at every surface via forced- and natural-convection coefficients, and models leaf boundary-layer resistance as a function of local wind speed. This makes ENVI-met uniquely capable of capturing the interplay between canopy shading, wind channeling through street canyons, and pedestrian-level thermal comfort. Computationally expensive; primarily used for small-domain (<1 km²) green infrastructure design.

SUEWS — Surface Urban Energy and Water Balance Scheme

A neighborhood- to city-scale surface energy balance model (100 m – 1 km) partitioning net radiation into sensible heat (QH), latent heat (QE), storage heat (ΔQS), and anthropogenic heat (QF). Convective exchange is parameterized through bulk aerodynamic transfer coefficients derived from urban roughness length and displacement height; the aerodynamic resistance (ra) linking surface to reference-level air adjusts for atmospheric stability (Monin–Obukhov similarity). ET is calculated via Penman–Monteith for each urban surface type (vegetation, pavement, water). Open-source and validated at flux tower sites globally; capable of multi-decadal runs.

WRF-Urban

A mesoscale model (1–10 km) coupling the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) atmosphere with urban canopy schemes (SLUCM, BEP, BEM). WRF-Urban resolves mesoscale wind circulations — urban–rural breezes, heat island–driven convergence zones, and nocturnal drainage flows — that govern city-scale advective heat transport. The Building Effect Parameterization (BEP) explicitly represents drag, turbulent kinetic energy, and convective fluxes within the urban canopy layer. Best suited for evaluating city-wide cooling strategies and projecting UHI intensity under future climate scenarios.

CLMU — Community Land Model Urban (CLM5 / CESM)

The urban module embedded in CLM5, representing cities as a canyon geometry (roof, sunlit/shaded walls, impervious road, pervious ground). It solves the full energy, water, and radiation balance for each surface type, with multi-layer thermal mass for building walls and roofs. Turbulent exchange between canyon surfaces and the overlying atmosphere is handled via CLM5’s surface-layer scheme (Zeng–Dickinson stability functions), coupling convective heat fluxes to the CESM atmosphere. Enables global/regional UHI simulation under CMIP6 SSP scenarios. Described in Oleson et al. (2010) and Lawrence et al. (2019).

TRM — Two-Resistance Mechanistic Model

A physically based analytical model that partitions urban vegetation cooling into latent and sensible heat fluxes via two resistances: aerodynamic resistance (ra, which encodes convective efficiency — governed by wind speed, roughness length, and atmospheric stability) and surface/stomatal resistance (rs, governed by LAI, VPD, and soil moisture):

\[\lambda E = \frac{\rho c_p}{\gamma} \cdot \frac{e_s(T_s) - e_a}{r_a + r_s}, \qquad H = \rho c_p \cdot \frac{T_s - T_a}{r_a}\]

The ra term explicitly links wind-driven convection to cooling: in low-wind, stable conditions ra rises and sensible heat dissipation is suppressed; in ventilated parks or street canyons with strong airflow, ra falls and convective cooling is enhanced. When rs is low (well-watered, open stomata), energy partitions to latent heat cooling; under drought stress rs rises and the burden shifts to convective sensible heat. Compatible with thermal remote sensing (Landsat, ECOSTRESS) for spatially continuous Ts inputs. Lightweight — runs at full satellite resolution city-wide without CFD or mesoscale compute cost.

i-Tree Eco

A USDA Forest Service tool that quantifies urban tree ecosystem services — cooling, carbon storage, air pollution removal, and stormwater interception — from field inventory data (species, DBH, crown dimensions) combined with local climate and hourly wind speed records. ET-based cooling is scaled to individual tree leaf area via a modified Penman–Monteith equation; sensible heat removal through convective exchange is estimated from crown surface area and local air temperature gradients. Outputs are expressed in physical units and monetary value (USD), making it the standard tool for city governments and urban forest managers.
