Process-Based Models

Process-based models (PBMs) — also called mechanistic or simulation models — represent ecosystem behavior through explicit mathematical formulations of physical, chemical, and biological processes. Rather than fitting empirical relationships to observed data, PBMs encode our understanding of how and why ecosystems function, using differential equations and energy/mass balance constraints derived from first principles.

Basic Mechanisms

Carbon and water cycling is typically represented through a coupled system: photosynthesis (GPP) is modeled using the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry (FvCB) biochemical scheme, which relates CO₂ assimilation to rubisco kinetics, electron transport capacity, and stomatal conductance (Ball–Berry or Medlyn models). GPP minus autotrophic respiration yields net primary production (NPP); NPP minus heterotrophic decomposition yields net ecosystem exchange (NEE). Soil carbon pools are represented as first-order decay compartments with temperature and moisture scalar modifiers (e.g., CENTURY, RothC). Water balance links potential evapotranspiration (Penman–Monteith) to soil moisture dynamics and runoff generation.

Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs) — such as LPJ-GUESS, CLM-FATES, ED2, ORCHIDEE, and JULES — simulate vegetation composition, carbon allocation, growth, mortality, and disturbance (fire, wind, insects) on a gridded domain, enabling climate–vegetation feedbacks at regional to global scales. Forest gap models (SORTIE, FORMIND) simulate individual tree birth, growth, competition, and death within small plots, capturing successional dynamics and size-structured competition. 3-PG (Physiological Principles Predicting Growth) is a simpler process-based model widely used in plantation forestry, driven by monthly climate inputs and parameterized from field measurements.

Advantages

  • Mechanistic interpretability — processes and parameters correspond directly to measurable biological quantities (Vcmax, wood density, decomposition rates)
  • Extrapolation to novel conditions — mechanistic constraints prevent unrealistic predictions under climate regimes outside the training data range, unlike empirical ML models
  • Captures feedbacks — vegetation–climate–soil feedbacks and their transient dynamics cannot be recovered from static empirical relationships
  • No labeled training data required — parameters are derived from physiological measurements and field experiments rather than large labeled observational datasets
  • Enables scenario analysis — consistent simulation framework allows comparison of SSP126 vs. SSP585, management interventions, or disturbance regimes under controlled conditions
  • Links scales — sub-daily physiological processes scale up to annual ecosystem carbon budgets through explicit mass balance bookkeeping

Disadvantages

  • Parameter uncertainty — many parameters (e.g., maximum carboxylation rate Vcmax, turnover times, allocation fractions) are poorly constrained globally and vary by species and climate
  • Structural uncertainty — different process formulations (e.g., stomatal models, mortality algorithms, fire modules) produce divergent predictions even when driven with identical inputs — a major source of inter-model spread in CMIP6
  • Computational cost — global DGVMs at fine resolution (0.5°–1°) require substantial computing resources; individual-based forest models become prohibitive at landscape scales
  • Validation challenges — process-level validation (e.g., leaf-scale photosynthesis vs. tower-scale NEE) is difficult; models may produce correct outputs for wrong reasons
  • Static species traits — most DGVMs use fixed plant functional type (PFT) parameterizations that do not capture intraspecific trait variability or evolutionary adaptation
  • Missing processes — nutrient cycling (especially phosphorus in tropics), mycorrhizal networks, insect herbivory, and hydraulic failure under drought are poorly represented in many models

Applications in Ecosystem Research

  • NPP projection under climate change — CMIP6 land surface models (CLM, JSBACH, ORCHIDEE) simulate future NPP trajectories under SSP126/SSP585 for attribution of IAV amplification
  • Forest carbon accounting — 3-PG and ED2 simulate plantation and natural forest growth and mortality for national greenhouse gas inventories
  • Post-disturbance succession — gap models simulate forest recovery trajectories after fire, logging, or insect outbreak over decadal to centennial time horizons
  • Water balance and drought stress — coupled water–carbon models (VIC, CLM) simulate soil moisture depletion, stomatal closure, and carbon assimilation decline during compound hot–dry events
  • Biomass estimation benchmarking — PBM-derived biomass maps serve as independent validation targets for remote sensing-based above-ground biomass products

Process-Based Models for Urban Cooling Effect Analysis

Urban green spaces regulate the thermal environment through several interacting physical mechanisms: evapotranspiration (latent heat flux that converts solar energy into water vapor rather than sensible heat), shading (reduction of shortwave radiation reaching the surface), albedo modification (vegetation albedo differs from impervious surfaces), and longwave radiation trapping (sky view factor effects within urban canyons). Process-based models simulate these mechanisms explicitly through coupled energy, water, and radiation balance equations, providing physically consistent estimates of cooling magnitude that complement empirical remote sensing analyses.

ENVI-met

ENVI-met is a three-dimensional microclimate simulation model designed specifically for the urban outdoor environment. It solves coupled atmospheric, radiative, and soil–vegetation equations at fine spatial scales (0.5–5 m), resolving the thermal interactions between buildings, paved surfaces, soil, and vegetation at the pedestrian level.

Developer: Michael Bruse / ENVI-met GmbH (University of Mainz origin) Scale: Microscale — individual streets, courtyards, parks (domain typically 100 m – 2 km) Key processes simulated: - Shortwave and longwave radiation (including shadowing by buildings and trees) - Sensible and latent heat fluxes from vegetated and impervious surfaces - Leaf-level transpiration and stomatal resistance - Turbulent wind flow and thermal plumes within the urban canopy - Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) and outdoor thermal comfort indices (PET, UTCI)

Advantages: Physically rigorous 3D radiation and turbulence; resolves individual tree crown geometry; widely validated for outdoor thermal comfort studies

Disadvantages: Computationally intensive for large domains; requires detailed 3D building and vegetation input data; not suited for city-wide or regional analysis

Applications: Quantifying cooling effect of street trees and parks at pedestrian scale; optimizing urban green infrastructure design; assessing outdoor heat stress under climate change scenarios

SUEWS — Surface Urban Energy and Water Balance Scheme

SUEWS is a neighborhood- to city-scale surface energy balance model that simulates the exchange of energy, water, and carbon between the urban surface and the atmosphere. It is designed to run over long time periods (years to decades) using standard meteorological inputs, making it well-suited for multi-year urban heat island analysis and green space cooling assessments.

Developer: Sue Grimmond’s group, King’s College London / University of Reading Scale: Neighborhood to city (~100 m – 1 km grid resolution) Key processes simulated: - Net radiation partitioning into sensible heat (QH), latent heat (QE), storage heat (ΔQS), and anthropogenic heat (QF) - Evapotranspiration from vegetated, paved, and water surfaces using a Penman–Monteith approach - Surface water balance (interception, soil moisture, runoff) - Urban morphology effects via sky view factor and plan area fractions of buildings, paving, vegetation, and water Key inputs: Meteorological forcing (air temperature, humidity, wind, radiation), land cover fractions, roughness parameters, vegetation LAI

Advantages: Long-term multi-year runs feasible; requires only standard meteorological inputs; open-source; handles mixed urban surface types; validated at dozens of flux tower sites globally

Disadvantages: Does not resolve within-grid spatial heterogeneity; no 3D radiative transfer; requires careful parameterization of urban morphology

Applications: Seasonal and interannual urban heat island magnitude; quantifying the evapotranspiration contribution of urban trees vs. grass; evaluating green roof and street tree cooling strategies over decadal timescales

WRF-Urban — Weather Research and Forecasting Model with Urban Canopy Parameterization

WRF (Weather Research and Forecasting) is a mesoscale numerical weather prediction model widely used for regional climate simulation. Its urban physics options — including the Single-Layer Urban Canopy Model (SLUCM), Multi-Layer Urban Canopy Model (BEP), and Building Energy Model (BEM) — represent urban surface energy exchanges and enable simulation of urban heat island dynamics at city to regional scale.

Developer: NCAR / NOAA / university consortium; urban physics by Martilli, Salamanca, and others Scale: Mesoscale — city to regional (1–10 km grid; can nest down to ~100 m) Key processes simulated: - Urban surface energy balance with building geometry (canyon width/height ratio, roof fraction) - Anthropogenic heat release from traffic and buildings - Radiative trapping in street canyons - Green space fraction effects on latent heat flux and surface temperature - Coupled atmosphere–surface feedback (urban heat island circulation)

Advantages: Captures urban–rural circulation and mesoscale UHI dynamics; accounts for atmospheric feedbacks absent in local-scale models; can downscale regional climate projections for urban impact assessment

Disadvantages: Requires substantial computational resources; urban canopy parameterization is simplified relative to microscale models; uncertainty in anthropogenic heat flux inputs; coarse representation of individual trees

Applications: City-scale urban heat island intensity under current and future climates; regional cooling effect of large urban forests and parks; urban planning scenarios (e.g., greening 10% of impervious surfaces) at city scale; heat wave amplification in urban areas

CLMU — Community Land Model Urban (within CLM5 / CESM)

CLMU (Community Land Model Urban) is the urban land surface scheme embedded within CLM5, the land component of the Community Earth System Model (CESM). Developed by Keith Oleson and colleagues at NCAR, CLMU represents urban areas as a simplified canyon geometry — a repeating unit of building roofs, sunlit and shaded walls, impervious road, and pervious ground (soil + vegetation) — and solves the full energy, water, and radiation balance for each surface type explicitly.

Developer: Keith Oleson et al., National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) Scale: Global / regional (grid resolution of CLM5: typically 0.5°–1°; urban fraction per grid cell) Canyon geometry representation: - Roof — building top surface with conduction through layers of construction material - Sunlit wall / shaded wall — vertical building facades with diurnal radiation cycling - Impervious road — paved canyon floor with heat storage in pavement layers - Pervious ground — soil and vegetated fraction within the canyon (grass, trees) - Anthropogenic heat flux — waste heat from traffic, HVAC, and metabolism added to canyon energy balance

Key processes simulated: - Multi-layer shortwave and longwave radiation exchange between canyon surfaces (multiple reflections between walls, road, and sky) - Sensible and latent heat fluxes from each urban surface type using separate resistance networks - Heat conduction through multi-layer building walls and roofs (urban thermal mass) - Snow accumulation and melt on roofs and roads - Runoff from impervious surfaces and infiltration into pervious ground - Vegetation transpiration within the canyon (Penman–Monteith, coupled to CLM5 plant hydraulics) - Urban–rural temperature contrast (UHI) emergent from coupled land–atmosphere simulation

Advantages: - Fully coupled to the CLM5 land surface and CESM atmosphere, enabling urban–climate feedbacks at regional and global scale - Physically consistent with the same carbon, water, and energy cycle representation as vegetated land in CLM5 - Can simulate future urban climate under CMIP6 SSP scenarios, including urban heat island amplification under warming - Multi-layer wall/roof thermal mass realistically captures the nocturnal heat release that drives nighttime UHI - Open-source and freely available as part of CESM

Disadvantages: - Canyon geometry is a strong simplification — does not resolve within-city spatial heterogeneity, tree crown geometry, or street orientation effects - Urban parameters (height-to-width ratio, roof fraction, wall/roof albedo and thermal properties) must be prescribed per city and are often uncertain - Coarse spatial resolution limits applicability to local-scale urban design or individual-park cooling analyses - Vegetation within the canyon uses simplified LAI prescriptions rather than dynamic growth

Applications: - Simulating global and regional urban heat island intensity under historical and future climate scenarios (SSP1-2.6 to SSP5-8.5) - Quantifying the contribution of urban green cover fraction to canyon latent heat flux and surface cooling within CESM - Benchmarking against flux tower observations in cities (e.g., ICOS urban sites) to evaluate urban energy balance closure - Assessing urban warming amplification beyond background climate change at continental scale

Note

CLMU is described in detail in Oleson et al. (2010) and updated for CLM5 in Lawrence et al. (2019). The model is freely available as part of CESM.

TRM — Two-Resistance Mechanistic Model

The Two-Resistance Mechanistic (TRM) model is a physically based framework for estimating the cooling effect of urban vegetation by explicitly representing the two resistances that govern heat and water vapour transfer between a vegetated surface and the overlying atmosphere: aerodynamic resistance (ra) and surface (stomatal) resistance (rs). It provides a process-level explanation of why and how much urban green spaces cool their surroundings, making it directly interpretable in terms of vegetation physiology and local micrometeorology.

Theoretical basis: Penman–Monteith evapotranspiration framework extended to urban canopy surfaces Scale: Leaf to canopy to urban patch (~1 m – 1 km, depending on parameterization) Developer: Widely applied framework derived from Monteith (1965); adapted to urban settings by multiple research groups in urban ecology and micrometeorology

Core mechanism — the two resistances:

The latent heat flux (evapotranspiration cooling) from urban vegetation is governed by:

\[\lambda E = \frac{\rho c_p}{\gamma} \cdot \frac{e_s(T_s) - e_a}{r_a + r_s}\]

And the sensible heat flux (warming of air) is:

\[H = \rho c_p \cdot \frac{T_s - T_a}{r_a}\]

Where:

Symbol Meaning
ra Aerodynamic resistance — governed by wind speed and surface roughness; controls turbulent transfer of heat and vapour between the canopy and reference height
rs Surface (stomatal) resistance — governed by leaf stomatal conductance, LAI, and vapour pressure deficit; controls how readily vegetation releases water vapour
Ts, Ta Surface and air temperature
es(Ts), ea Saturation vapour pressure at surface temperature; actual vapour pressure of ambient air
ρ, cp, γ Air density, specific heat, and psychrometric constant

The key insight: when rs is low (well-watered, open stomata, high LAI), most available energy is partitioned into latent heat (cooling); when rs is high (drought stress, low LAI), energy goes into sensible heat (warming). ra controls how efficiently that vapour escapes to the atmosphere — denser canopies and calm winds increase ra, limiting cooling even when stomata are open.

Key processes simulated: - Partitioning of net radiation into latent (QE) and sensible (QH) heat via resistance analogy - Stomatal response to vapour pressure deficit, air temperature, solar radiation, and soil moisture - Leaf area index (LAI) scaling from leaf-level to canopy-level resistance - Feedback between surface cooling and the vapour pressure gradient driving ET - Remote sensing integration — ra derived from wind profiles; Ts from thermal infrared imagery (Landsat, MODIS, ECOSTRESS); LAI from NDVI/Sentinel-2

Advantages: - Mechanistically interpretable — cooling magnitude is directly linked to measurable biophysical quantities (LAI, stomatal conductance, wind speed, soil moisture) - Remote sensing compatible — surface temperature (Ts) from thermal infrared satellites can drive or validate the model without requiring detailed meteorological tower networks - Computationally lightweight — analytical solution; runs at high spatial resolution over large cities without the computational cost of 3D CFD or mesoscale models - Applicable to species and structure differences — rs varies by tree species and drought status, enabling species-level cooling comparisons - Widely validated — extensive literature across tropical, temperate, and arid urban systems

Disadvantages: - Assumes well-mixed single layer — does not resolve vertical structure within the canopy or between-building airflow (no 3D radiative transfer or turbulence) - Requires accurate Ts and meteorological inputs — errors in aerodynamic resistance estimation (particularly in low-wind or complex-terrain urban settings) propagate into ET estimates - Drought stress parameterization — soil moisture dependence of rs requires either in situ soil measurements or model-assimilated products - No building shading effects — pure resistance framework does not account for radiation reduction by adjacent structures, which must be handled separately

Applications in urban cooling research: - Quantifying the evapotranspiration component of park and street tree cooling separately from shading effects - Mapping spatial variation in urban tree cooling efficiency as a function of species composition, canopy cover, and water availability using thermal remote sensing (Landsat/ECOSTRESS) - Attributing observed park cool island intensity to stomatal vs. aerodynamic resistance controls under heat wave conditions - Evaluating the effect of drought stress on urban tree cooling capacity — when rs rises under water deficit, cooling potential declines even if canopy cover is maintained - Benchmarking against ENVI-met and SUEWS outputs to isolate the ET cooling signal from the total urban energy balance

i-Tree Eco — Urban Forest Ecosystem Services Model

i-Tree Eco is a peer-reviewed urban forestry modeling tool developed by the USDA Forest Service that quantifies the ecosystem services provided by urban trees — including air temperature reduction, air pollution removal, stormwater interception, and carbon storage — using field inventory data combined with local climate and pollution data.

Developer: USDA Forest Service, Davey Tree Expert Company, and partners Scale: Plot to city (based on field-sampled urban tree inventory) Key processes simulated: - Transpiration-based cooling using a modified Penman–Monteith equation scaled to leaf area - Shading of buildings and pavement (reduction in building energy use) - Carbon storage and annual sequestration from tree growth - Air pollution deposition (O₃, NO₂, SO₂, PM₂.₅) to leaf surfaces - Stormwater interception by tree canopy

Advantages: Directly links field-measured tree attributes (DBH, height, species, crown dimensions) to ecosystem service estimates; outputs in monetary value (USD) for policy communication; free and well-documented; widely used by city governments and NGOs

Disadvantages: Cooling estimates are indirect (energy savings proxy rather than direct surface temperature simulation); does not simulate urban atmospheric dynamics or spatial temperature patterns; accuracy depends on quality of field inventory

Applications: Quantifying the economic value of urban tree cooling services; supporting urban forest management decisions; estimating cooling by species and size class; benchmarking remote sensing-derived cooling estimates against process-based tree-level calculations
